\section[Introduction: the honest starting point]{Introduction: Why the Straight Line Is Still the Honest Starting Point, Two Hundred Years On}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The questions this tutorial answers}

Every machine learning course and every statistics textbook teaches linear regression in the first two weeks, and almost all of them teach it the same way: you are handed a formula
\[
  \widehat{\bm{\beta}} = (\X^\top \X)^{-1} \X^\top \y,
\]
told to memorize it, and then rushed off toward ``more exciting'' models.

This tutorial does the opposite: every step is earned from scratch. By the end, you should be able to answer, from first principles:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item Why is the slope of one-dimensional regression $\dfrac{\Cov(x,y)}{\Var(x)}$? What exactly is its relationship to the Pearson correlation coefficient? (Chapter~\ref{sec:onedim})
  \item Why can a straight line measure the \textit{correlation} between $x$ and $y$, yet tell you nothing about the \textit{direction} of causation? (Chapter~\ref{sec:onedim})
  \item Why squared error? What exactly would be lost with a different loss? (Section~\ref{sec:why-square})
  \item In high dimensions, when the sample size $n$ far exceeds the dimension $d$, why does least squares admit the closed-form solution $\widehat{\bm{\beta}} = (\X^\top\X)^{-1}\X^\top\y$? When is this solution unique, and when is it optimal? (Chapter~\ref{sec:highdim})
  \item When $n$ no longer dwarfs $d$, what breaks first, and what repairs it? (Section~\ref{sec:hd-break})
  \item Putting the closed form and the gradient-descent iteration side by side, what is the exact solution of $\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_t$? What does the fixed point mean? (Chapter~\ref{sec:gd})
  \item When $d > n$ and the solutions form a manifold, where does the iteration converge? How is it related to the pseudoinverse solution? (Chapter~\ref{sec:gd-underdet})
  \item How are regression coefficients related to the covariance and precision matrices? How does ``estimation'' graduate into ``inference'' --- from estimate to inference? (Chapter~\ref{sec:ggm})
  \item When the observed features are in fact nonlinear projections of latent variables, why does linear regression behave as a kernel method? (Chapter~\ref{sec:kernel})
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A two-hundred-year-old idea that refuses to retire}

Least squares was first published by Legendre (1805) and independently proposed by Gauss (1809), who gave it a full probabilistic derivation and used it to predict the orbit of Ceres from noisy astronomical observations\cite{legendre1805,gauss1809}. The method has outlived every paradigm shift since: the computer, the bootstrap, the deep learning revolution. For exactly one reason:

\begin{keybox}[The thesis of this tutorial]
Linear regression is popular not because the world is linear --- it rarely is. It is popular because:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item a local linear approximation is often good enough (Taylor's theorem guarantees that every smooth function is approximately linear locally);
  \item it is the \textit{only} baseline that is exactly solvable, interpretable, and auditable --- every other model measures itself against it;
  \item it is the foundation of logistic regression, generalized linear models, kernel methods, and even the output layer of neural networks.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Prerequisites}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Calculus}: partial derivatives, the chain rule, and the fact that extrema of smooth functions occur where derivatives vanish;
  \item \textbf{Linear algebra}: vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication, transpose, inverse; the reading of ``matrix times vector as a linear combination of columns'';
  \item \textbf{Probability} (used from Chapter~2 onward): expectation, variance, covariance, correlation.
\end{itemize}

Everything else is derived in the text.

\subsection{Notation, stated once}

One-dimensional case: $n$ paired observations $(x_i, y_i) \in \R^2$, $i = 1, \dots, n$. High-dimensional case: responses $y_i \in \R$, features $\x_i = (x_{i1}, \dots, x_{id})^\top \in \R^d$, design matrix $\X \in \R^{n \times d}$ (rows equal to $\x_i^\top$), parameters $\bbeta \in \R^d$. The error terms $\varepsilon_i$ absorb everything a hyperplane cannot explain: measurement noise, omitted variables, genuine randomness, and the original sin of approximating a nonlinear world with a hyperplane.

Conventions:
\[
  \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \qquad
  \Var_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2, \qquad
  \Cov_n(x, y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}),
\]
i.e., sample variance and covariance with denominator $n$ (using $n-1$ would only introduce an immaterial factor into every ratio). Throughout, $\Var$ and $\Cov$ without a subscript denote population quantities taken with respect to expectation, and the subscript $n$ denotes sample quantities.
